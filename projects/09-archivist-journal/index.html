The Silence of Cast Concrete: The Spatial Poetics of Monolithic Enclosure

By Valentina Mori, Ph.D. — Illustrated by H. Richter

To enter Tadao Ando’s Church of the Light in Ibaraki at mid-morning is not to observe an architectural composition, but to be absorbed into a silent dialogue between gravity and sheer radiance. There are no ornamental distractions, no moldings, no polychrome mosaics. There is only the cool gray plane of vibrated concrete, punctuated by a cruciate void through which raw sunlight pierces the gloom.

"Architecture is not about creating novel geometries. It is about constructing a sanctuary in which the human consciousness can hear itself breathe."

When Peter Zumthor cast the Bruder Klaus Field Chapel in Mechernich, Germany, he constructed a conical teepee of 112 tree trunks harvested from local forests, layered concrete over them in twenty-four distinct 50-centimeter pours over twenty-four days, and then set a slow smoldering fire inside the interior for three continuous weeks. The wood burned away, leaving behind charred, soot-blackened flutes in the concrete walls that smell perpetually of cedar smoke and damp limestone.

In our contemporary era of ephemeral glass curtain walls and algorithmically modulated LED facades, monolithic mass possesses an almost transgressive stillness. Concrete, in its fluid origin state, is liquid stone. It assumes the texture of whatever timber containment cradles it. When cured, it preserves the grain of the wood long after the tree has decayed into humus. It is a photographic emulsion cast in stone.
